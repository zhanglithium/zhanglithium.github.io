# Reconstructing and Mapping Historical Air Pollution Inequality: Data, Methods, and Digital Representation
1. Demetillo et al. (2020) — Observing Nitrogen Dioxide Air Pollution Inequality Using High-Spatial-Resolution Remote Sensing Measurements in Houston, Texas
Demetillo, M. A. G., et al. (2020). “Observing Nitrogen Dioxide Air Pollution Inequality Using High-Spatial-Resolution Remote Sensing Measurements in Houston, Texas.” Environmental Science & Technology, 54(16), 9882–9895. https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.0c01864
This study uses high-resolution airborne NO2 observations and satellite measurements to investigate air pollution inequality in Houston, Texas. Remote sensing technology together with demographic data is applied to identify pollution inequalities between communities. I selected this research paper as the ground for our research work conducted by the group. The use of satellite observations compared to TROPOMI data shows the significance of remote sensing in revealing the pollution inequality at the neighborhood level, as well as the significance of spatial resolution and observational uncertainty of the study outcomes. The insights gained have a direct impact on my research work on reconstructing past high-resolution NO2 patterns and assessment of the opportunities for doing research in the area of environmental inequality studies.
2. Demetillo et al. (2021) — Space-Based Observational Constraints on NO2 Air Pollution Inequality From Diesel Traffic in Major US Cities
Demetillo, M. A. G., Harkins, C., McDonald, B. C., Chodrow, P. S., Sun, K., & Pusede, S. E. (2021). “Space-Based Observational Constraints on NO2 Air Pollution Inequality From Diesel Traffic in Major US Cities.” Geophysical Research Letters, 48(17), e2021GL094333. https://doi.org/10.1029/2021GL094333
This paper uses TROPOMI satellite observations to investigate NO2 inequality in 52 major U.S. cities. The authors combine population-weighted satellite measurements with demographic and emissions data to identify unequal pollution distributions and their possible sources. They find an important contribution from diesel traffic to urban NO2 inequality. I chose this study because it is closely related to the original motivation for my research. Our project aims to reconstruct higher-resolution NO2 data for earlier years, extending the temporal coverage available for investigating similar questions. The paper also provides useful methods for calculating inequality and comparing pollution differences across cities.
3. Di et al. (2020) — Assessing NO2 Concentration and Model Uncertainty with High Spatiotemporal Resolution
Di, Q., et al. (2020). “Assessing NO2 Concentration and Model Uncertainty with High Spatiotemporal Resolution across the Contiguous United States Using Ensemble Model Averaging.” Environmental Science & Technology, 54(3), 1372–1384. https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.9b03358
Di et al. used multiple machine learning algorithms for estimating daily NO2 concentrations throughout the United States at high spatial resolution. The proposed model incorporates environmental variables and assesses the uncertainty in its predictions. I chose this article because it illustrates the use of machine learning for reconstructing historical records of air pollution at locations other than those where measurements have been collected. It is also a good example of the use of model-generated data for environmental and health research. Their study addresses near-surface NO2 concentrations, while my study utilizes satellite-derived NO2 columns. The parameters are different, but the study demonstrates practical methodological approaches that can be used for model evaluation, uncertainty analysis, and reconstruction of historical data for research purposes.
4. Clark, Millet, and Marshall (2014) — National Patterns in Environmental Injustice and Inequality
Clark, L. P., Millet, D. B., & Marshall, J. D. (2014). “National Patterns in Environmental Injustice and Inequality: Outdoor NO2 Air Pollution in the United States.” PLOS ONE, 9(4), e94431. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094431
This study investigates environmental inequality in the United States by combining outdoor NO2 concentration estimates with demographic data. The authors identify differences in air pollution associated with race and socioeconomic characteristics. I chose this paper because investigating historical air pollution inequality is one of the original motivations for my research. It provides an example of moving beyond pollution concentration maps to examine how pollution is distributed across different populations. My reconstructed historical NO2 data could eventually support similar spatial comparisons over longer periods. However, satellite column measurements are not direct measures of personal exposure, so additional data and careful interpretation would be required.
5. D'Ignazio and Klein (2020) — Data Feminism
D'Ignazio, C., & Klein, L. F. (2020). Data Feminism. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/11805.001.0001
Data Feminism studies the link between data science and inequality. The authors state that the collection, analysis, and presentation of data are not neutral processes. I chose this book because I want to study environmental inequality using satellite data. The scientific measurements do not describe the social inequality themselves. It is up to researchers to determine which groups to compare, how to delineate geographical areas, and how to interpret the differences. The choices made here are important for what conclusions will be made in maps and statistics. The book provides a theoretical context for understanding what the social implications of environmental data analysis are and how to design maps communicating inequalities responsibly.
6. McDonough (2024) — Maps as Data
McDonough, K. (2024). “Maps as Data.” In Computational Humanities. University of Minnesota Press. Read online
Course syllabus — Week 5
McDonough discusses how digitization and computational methods change the ways researchers work with historical maps. Maps can become datasets through georeferencing, extracting information, and applying computational analysis. I chose this chapter because maps are central to my research, both for analyzing reconstructed NO2 patterns and for presenting environmental inequality. The chapter made me consider the relationship between maps and the data they represent. A map is not simply an image of research results. Its spatial boundaries, resolution, and organization influence how information can be interpreted and compared. These ideas are relevant to my plan to create a digital collection of historical pollution inequality maps.
7. Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya (2026) — Computational Approaches to Cultural Heritage in the Global South
Murrieta-Flores, P., & Shanmugapriya, T. (2026). “Computational Approaches to Cultural Heritage in the Global South: Infrastructures, Analytics, and Epistemic Justice.” Journal of Cultural Analytics, 11(2), 1–13. https://doi.org/10.22148/jca.1510
Course syllabus — Week 2
This article examines computational approaches to cultural heritage and emphasizes that datasets are shaped by the processes of collection, organization, and interpretation. The authors discuss projects that reconstruct historical and spatial information from fragmented records, including participatory mapping practices. I chose this reading because it connects data construction with knowledge production. My research also attempts to create information that is not directly available in existing observations, although its data sources and research methods are different. The article encourages me to consider how the process of building a dataset affects the questions researchers can ask and the people who may benefit from the results.
8. Boggs, Reed, and Purdom Lindblad (2017) — Making It Matter
Boggs, J., Reed, J., & Purdom Lindblad, J. K. (2017). “Making It Matter.” In Making Things and Drawing Boundaries: Experiments in the Digital Humanities. University of Minnesota Press. Read online
Course syllabus — Week 6
This chapter discusses critical making and emphasizes inclusiveness, transparency, and accountability in digital projects. The authors argue that making should consider the people involved and the social effects of its products. I selected this reading because I plan to build a website presenting historical environmental inequality maps. The project requires decisions about data selection, map design, accessibility, and the information provided to readers. These decisions affect how people understand and use the results. The chapter provides a useful framework for treating website development as part of the research process rather than simply a way to publish finished scientific figures.
9. Nelson, Winling, et al. (2023) — Mapping Inequality: Redlining in New Deal America
Nelson, R. K., Winling, L., et al. (2023). Mapping Inequality: Redlining in New Deal America. American Panorama, Digital Scholarship Lab, University of Richmond. https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining
Digital Humanities Project
Mapping Inequality, a project that deals in presenting historical maps along with related details and spatial information. It serves as a platform for understanding the redlining practices in various cities throughout the United States. The reason for selecting this project was because it uses digital mapping techniques to convey the idea of historical inequalities to the general public, while I focus on air pollution problems. The fact that it presents a mix of interactive maps, historical context, and useful data is an important aspect of the project that can help in organizing and presenting our website. It also shows the significance of providing an explanation of historical facts instead of relying on maps only.
10. Roth (2013) — Interactive Maps: What We Know and What We Need to Know
Roth, R. E. (2013). “Interactive Maps: What We Know and What We Need to Know.” Journal of Spatial Information Science, 6, 59–115. https://doi.org/10.5311/JOSIS.2013.6.105
Roth reviews the theory and design of interactive maps, including how users interact with geographic information through digital interfaces. The article examines the issue of interaction: when it is advantageous, when it should be used and what impact it may have on map interpretation. I decided to use Roth’s article as my source because I am planning to create a CollectionBuilder website that studies historical pollution inequality. The site would allow users to search through maps by cities and years and see how this data varies across communities. Roth’s analysis provides grounds for choosing the types of interactions to offer to the readers of my maps. In addition, I was reminded that creating interactive maps requires good design rather than just adding more and more functions.

Source Selection Rationale
My research involves integrating satellite remote sensing, machine learning, spatial analysis, and environmental inequality. I chose these ten references to link the technical aspects of my project with the bigger issues concerning the use of data and how the findings of research are reported.
Initially, I focused on works produced by our research group, which did a lot of work with satellite observations regarding the inequality of NO2 air pollution. I chose a couple of studies published in 2020 and 2021 because they form the basis of what I am currently investigating. Besides, they argue for the need for improvement of both the spatial resolution and historical depth of satellite observations in regard to environmental inequality research.
I further identified new academic literature on machine learning, NO2 reconstruction, and environmental inequality. Journal websites and other references in the literature were utilized to find sources that could be useful for research methodologies and ways to apply them.
In the end, I found 3 readings from the course syllabus, the Data Feminism reading outside the syllabus, and 2 other articles related to digital mapping and visualization. These readings gave me ideas on how to relate my research to Digital Humanities. I plan to build a website with historical environmental inequality maps using CollectionBuilder software. The readings and the digital project supply some good ideas about the data organization, the map design, and the public availability of the research done.
