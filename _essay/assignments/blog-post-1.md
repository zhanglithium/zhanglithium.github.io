## What Can Machine Learning Make Visible? From Colonial Archives to Environmental Inequality

I initially thought of data injustice in terms of absent information or unequal data access. However, Patricia Murrieta-Flores's work on colonial Latin America archives exposed me to a more complicated problem: information can exist, but how it is recorded, conserved and processed can prevent us from using it. While her research uses machine learning algorithms for extracting transcriptions from historical manuscripts, her lecture presents it not only as a technological achievement but also as a tool that can help us rediscover people and events that had disappeared.

In Computational Approaches to Cultural Heritage in the Global South, Patricia Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya (2026) argue that data is not something that just exists. Data arises from digital infrastructure, institutions and communities involved in its recording. One example discussed in the article reconstructs the routes of dance troupes in India using diaries, receipts, and interviews. Here, building a spatial dataset is itself part of the research, rather than merely preparing information for later analysis. This example made me think about how limitations in data can shape the questions asked by researchers.

I face a similar challenge in my own research, although it’s about air pollution rather than historical archives. I use machine learning to reconstruct finer-scale patterns of nitrogen dioxide (NO2) from satellite observations. One original motivation for my project was to investigate air pollution inequality over past decades. High resolution satellite observation is only available in recent years, while earlier satellite observations have relatively coarse spatial resolution that can not reveal neighborhood-level inequalities. Machine learning could provide a way to reconstruct high resolution air pollution records back in time by learning relationships between recent products. Then evaluate the historical air pollution inequality.

However, this connection also makes me more cautious about the meaning of data injustice. A model-generated high-resolution map is not the same as a direct high-resolution observation. It may reveal useful patterns, but it may also introduce errors or produce details that the original measurements cannot support. Moreover, observing NO2 patterns is not equivalent to measuring people's exposure or proving environmental injustice. Those questions also require demographic information, additional evidence, and careful interpretation.

Murrieta-Flores's research on decolonial technologies raises another important question: who designs these tools, and who benefits from their results? For me, machine learning can help confront data injustice by making previously obscured information easier to study. But the goal should not simply be to produce more data or sharper maps. It should be to create knowledge that is trustworthy, useful, and open to critical examination.
