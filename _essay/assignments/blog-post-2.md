# Mapping Historical Air Pollution Inequality: Digital Methods in My Research
My research uses machine learning to reconstruct high-resolution nitrogen dioxide (NO2) from satellite observations back to 2004. One motivation is to investigate intraurban air pollution inequality over decades. As before 2018, only low-resolution NO2 products are available and do not have the ability to observe the neighborhood-level inequality. The reconstructed data could support historical air pollution inequality research, but the next challenge is how to turn these data into maps that people can understand and use.
Discussion on Maps and (Counter) Mapping for Week 5 provoked me to reconsider the process of mapping. I can reflect on it by considering the role of maps as sources rather than just images. The same question applies to my research but in a different way: how to turn analytical data into maps that will enable us to understand the data better? A map showing NO2 concentrations can indicate the areas where pollution is more or less concentrated but cannot show the unequal distribution of pollution. To find out how unequal pollution is, one should compare pollution data to information about the affected people and communities.
I intend to utilize my reconstructed NO2 dataset along with demographic and social-economic data to investigate the variations among neighborhoods and how they have evolved over the decades. For example, maps could depict the history of pollution levels in various areas of a city and additional data allow the audience to determine whether certain communities experienced continuously high levels of pollution. However, any conclusions drawn from these comparisons must be done with caution because satellite NO2 column data does not show pollution levels for private exposure but just represents a calculation rather than a historical record.
I have a vision to develop a CollectionBuilder website featuring a particular emphasis on air pollution inequality maps. My intention is to compile instances of inequality categorized by city and year while providing maps with concise descriptions, information on data sources, and access to methodology. Users of my site would be able to navigate different time frames, analyze distinct communities, and connect the information with bigger questions on environmental inequality. Each map will feature some key information on spatial resolution, availability of data, and uncertainty. Furthermore, I will differentiate among first-gathered satellite measurements and reconstructed results. My primary goal, however, is to offer a more useful resource beyond visual illustrations of my findings, transforming many of the reconstruction data into something usable for many people out of atmospheric science.
The readings for Week 6 regarding Critical Making complement the project. Salter and Johnson’s work on Map, and the contribution from Boggs et al. on Making It Matter, got me thinking about the choices made regarding the development of digital projects. In developing the website, decisions have to be made about the cities that will be included, how neighborhood borders are defined, how to show differences of color and how uncertain data can be displayed. Such choices have an impact on what message the map conveys. I want the website to facilitate the examination of unequal patterns of pollution, without converting estimates into oversimplified assumptions. The importance of digital methods lies in their ability to generate data and, at the same time, determine the manner in which the issue of environmental injustice can be studied and conveyed.
